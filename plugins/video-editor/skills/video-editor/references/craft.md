# Craft rules for short talking-head videos

House rules this editor follows. They come from six short-form creators' published hook frameworks (Kallaway's triple hook, Jun Yuh's three-part ten-second hook, Chris Chung's hook, primer and super hook, Jen Gottlieb's HOTTI, Anci's six-step structure and Kierza Porter's retention script), Instagram's own notes on what it ranks, and a working editor's review notes. Any skip rate, watch time or view count a creator quotes is their own claim, not platform data: treat it as a reason to test, never as a promise to the person.

## The first three seconds (the Hook Stack)

Stack these in the opening, in this order:

1. **Interrupt on frame 0.** Something is already moving (the editor's settle-zoom does this) with a sound at 0:00. Never a logo, a greeting or a title card.
2. **Text hook on screen from frame 0.** Seven words or fewer, in bold on solid bars, readable on frame 0. It names the viewer's problem or the outcome they want, often with a negative word ("stop", "never", "wrong", "dying"). It is the headline, not a copy of the spoken line.
   - Good: "Still doing your / taxes in April?" over "Most small business owners pay more than they need to."
   - Weak: "3 tips for plants" (a topic, not a problem); "Hi, I'm Dana" (a greeting).
3. **Context in the first spoken sentence.** The topic is unmistakable straight away. If the take opens slowly ("So, um, today I wanted to talk about…"), start the cut at the first line that says something.
4. **Lean, then snap.** Pull one way, then twist ("it's not your prices, it's your follow-up").
5. **Proof, only if it's real.** One honest line about results, effort or research, if the person said one. Never invent it; skip the layer when there isn't one.
6. **Open loop.** The body pays off what the opener promised, quickly.

**The 3-second test:** from the first three seconds alone, a stranger knows what it's about, why it matters to them and what they're waiting to find out.

**Frame 0 does the hijacking.** The first frame is the most vivid in the video and never dark: the hook bars are on screen, the picture is already moving (the settle-zoom), and a sound hits at 0:00. A viewer scrolling fast gives you about two seconds.

**Hook type hierarchy.** A small setup line over a punch line about twice its size; the punch line slams in. Never two equal bars, never a fade-in.

**Test hooks.** Offer three hook options per video. When the person posts, they can make three versions that differ only in the first three seconds, post them as Instagram trial reels (shown first to people who don't follow them yet), and keep the one people skip less. Suggest they keep a simple list: hook, skip rate, average watch time. A flop gets a new first three seconds, not a new video.

## Pace

- Cut every pause longer than about 0.35 s inside a line; leave about 0.4 s after the strongest line and a settled hold of about a second after the last word. Never end on the last syllable.
- Something changes every 1.5-3 s in the first ten seconds: a cut, a zoom or a caption beat.
- A zoom on every cut hides the jump (alternate about 1.04 and 1.12, never past 1.15), with a slow push between cuts.
- Keep the person's spoken order. A strong later line can open the video only when they agree.

## Captions

- Every spoken word is captioned, so the video works on mute. Two or three words at a time, at most 16 letters, appearing as they're said.
- Key words get the brand colour: the number, the problem, the fix. Two to five per video, never filler.
- Captions hide while the same words are on screen big (the hook uses different words, so captions stay on under it).
- Captions break at every cut and every pause, stay on the right words, and never cover the mouth.
- Safe zone on a 1080×1920 frame: nothing important in the top 220 px, the bottom 420 px or the right 140 px, where Instagram and TikTok draw their buttons. (A conservative house rule from creator guides, not a published platform number.)

## Sound

- Sound effects mark a visual change only: an impact or pop on the hook, a whoosh on a zoom or wipe, a soft click on a key word, a chime ringing into the end. About eight in a 40-second video at most; a run of the same sound counts as one. If it sounds like a meme edit, it's too much.
- Each effect starts early by the time its loudest moment takes, so the hit lands exactly on the picture change.
- The voice is the star: effects sit well under it.
- Master to -14 LUFS with the true peak at or under -1 dBTP, the level Instagram, TikTok and YouTube Shorts play at. The editor masters to -1.5 dBTP to leave headroom.

## Graphics and transitions

- Illustrate the idea, not every sentence: 4 to 8 visual beats in a 30-60 s video, each explaining a concept or highlighting the key phrase. If the only reason for a graphic is a noun the person said (a place, an object), cut it.
- At most two transition types per video: the zoom on every cut, plus one accent (wipe or flash) at the story's turns. Jump cuts get zooms, not blur whips.
- Every graphic carries its own contrast: words on chips or plates, key-word chips with a white outline. Nothing stays on screen under 0.6 s.

## Restraint

- At most one big text element on screen besides the captions.
- No watermark, borders, letterboxing, "link in bio" card or fake progress bar.
- No generated footage, stock clips or music without the rights.
- Never put words in the captions the person didn't say, and never invent results.

## Check your own work

- Check the finished file, not the plan: the stills at frame 0, either side of every cut, each highlighted word and the end.
- A second pair of eyes that didn't build the edit catches what you miss (on one real edit, a fresh reviewer found eight problems in the first build). When you can, have a subagent read only the QA stills and list flaws with timecodes.
- Fix every FAIL, at most three rounds; then say plainly what's left.
